    Learned Error Diagnosis for GEPA's Reflection

    August 18, 2026 Andrei Cojocaru*, Lakshya A Agrawal*, Joseph E. Gonzalez, Dan Klein, Koushik Sen, Alexandros G. Dimakis, Ion Stoica, Matei Zaharia
    * Equal Contribution

    Learned Error Diagnosis for GEPA's Reflection¶

    TL;DR. GEPA's reflection model proposes new prompts by analyzing a program's executions and evaluator feedback. We give it more context: an LLM judge automatically identifies concrete problems in those executions, reporting what went wrong, where it happened, and the supporting evidence. Providing these findings improved held-out scores by up to 9.7 percentage points over standard GEPA under the same optimization budget.

    During each GEPA optimization round, a candidate is evaluated on a small set of tasks, and the results go to a reflection model, the LLM that proposes a revised prompt. For the step being revised, it sees the task, that step's output, and the evaluator's feedback. Evaluation tells reflection how far the output fell short and what is missing: the correct answer, the gold documents not yet retrieved, the constraints that failed. We introduce an LLM judge that reads the whole run and tells reflection what each step did wrong: a named failure pattern, with evidence from the trace.

    The judge uses a fixed vocabulary to group individual failures instead of reporting each one separately in free form. By naming the common denominator among its findings, we give reflection a broader perspective on the overall cause of a failure instead of having it fixate on individual instances. We call this vocabulary a failure taxonomy: a short list of the ways this particular program fails, built automatically from its own executions. Each entry is a failure mode, a failure pattern with a name and a description.1

    The judge works from the trace alone; the task's score, gold answer, and evaluator feedback are withheld from it. Keeping the outcome hidden prevents the result from influencing which problems it looks for. A program may make a mistake at an intermediate step and still reach the correct answer through a later recovery or a fortunate guess. Outcome-blind analysis therefore draws useful information from successful and unsuccessful executions alike, giving the reflection model a fuller account of how the candidate behaves.

    Throughout, GEPA's evaluator keeps its role: the task score still decides which candidates survive, and the judge's findings only inform the reflection model. Figure 1 shows the resulting held-out scores.

    How it works¶

    Our approach consists of two stages: constructing a failure taxonomy tailored to the program and its use case, then using an LLM judge to apply that taxonomy throughout optimization and provide findings to the reflection model.

    Taxonomy generation¶

    Building the vocabulary takes two inputs, the program and a dataset. The same launcher that starts optimization runs the base program over the dataset, collects the trajectories, and hands them to AdaMAST2, which generates the taxonomy on its own. The categories, the labels, and the adjudication are all produced by the pipeline itself. Inside AdaMAST, the process runs as follows.

    AdaMAST first analyzes the trajectories to understand the program's domain, structure, and component roles, as well as the behavioral patterns present in its executions. It uses this analysis to draft system-level, role-specific, and domain-specific failure modes. The draft is then consolidated to remove overlapping modes, clarify their boundaries, and identify gaps in coverage.

    The resulting taxonomy then passes through agreement rounds. In each round, four LLM annotators (four separate contexts of one model) read the same five trajectories, agree on which errors are present, and independently assign one or more failure modes to each. AdaMAST then measures how consistently they chose the same modes (macro Fleiss' κ over the failure modes used) and how much of what they found the taxonomy covers.

    Between rounds, while κ is below its target, the taxonomy is refined: the definitions of the modes the annotators disagreed on are clarified. The process stops once κ has stayed at or above 0.75 and coverage at or above 0.70 for three consecutive rounds, or at the round cap.

    Once the rounds finish, the taxonomy is frozen and reused throughout optimization and across other runs of the same program. The companion pipeline additionally trims a freshly generated taxonomy to the failure modes its own traces support before freezing it, a step added after the study.

    In our experiments, each benchmark's taxonomy took four or five agreement rounds and ended with 22 to 28 failure modes (Table 1).

    Judging during optimization¶

    GEPA captures the execution traces of every candidate it evaluates during optimization, and the LLM judge reviews each trace against the frozen taxonomy. It sees the task and every step's inputs, prompts, and outputs.

    For each execution, the judge records every finding: the failure mode, the step where it occurred, and the supporting evidence. This is a dedicated pass over the trajectory, on top of the reflective examples the adapter builds, and it surfaces failure modes those examples do not spell out. One execution can show several failure modes, and the same failure mode more than once.

    In our runs, as in GEPA's default, one step (a component, in its terms) is revised at a time, and reflection now sees the judge's findings for that step alongside the usual inputs, outputs, and feedback. Figure 2 shows where the judge sits in one reflection step.

    Same budget, different allocation¶

    The judge is the only operation added during optimization, and it is paid for from the same budget. Every baseline and judge run received $60 per seed, covering the optimization loop only: minibatch rollouts, reflection, validation of promoted candidates, and, in the judge arm, the judge, which uses the same model as reflection. The base program's initial validation and the final test evaluation are outside the allocated budget, for both arms. Candidate selection, sampling, scheduling, and the rule for accepting a proposal are identical in both arms.

    The judge arm proposed fewer candidates, since part of its budget went to the judge, and accepted a larger share of them: 50.8% overall, against 45.1% for standard GEPA. Its accepted-candidate pool therefore stayed close in size to the baseline's. Matched budgets and a similar accepted pool rule out the two obvious alternative explanations for the gap: that the judge arm simply spent more, or that it had more accepted candidates to choose from.

    Table 2 separates the matched online budget from one-time offline preparation. Trace harvesting runs the base program once over a chosen trace source (the validation split in our runs) and records the inputs and outputs of every step. Those model calls cost $1.14 for HotpotQA, $1.87 for IFBench, and $1.28 for HoVer.

    Generating and refining the taxonomy costs $3 to $4 per benchmark. Combined with trace harvesting, the one-time offline setup costs $4 to $6 per benchmark, or roughly 3% of the $180 optimization budget across three judge-arm seeds. The frozen taxonomy can then be reused across seeds and later runs.

    Results¶

    We evaluated on three benchmarks: multi-hop question answering (HotpotQA), instruction following (IFBench), and multi-hop claim verification (HoVer). Each arm ran three seeds.

    Setup
    • Models. Haiku 4.5 as the task model; Sonnet 4.6 for both GEPA's reflection model and the judge.
    • Splits. 150 train / 300 validation / 300 test on all three. HotpotQA is drawn from the fullwiki validation set (7,405 labeled examples), HoVer from the dev release (4,000 claims), stratified by hop count (2, 3, or 4).
    • IFBench generalization. Train and validation come from allenai/IF_multi_constraints_upto5; the test set is all 300 examples of allenai/IFBench_test. The constraint vocabularies are disjoint, with 54 constraint IDs on the train side and 58 on the test side, so the result cannot come from replaying a train-side constraint ID.
    • Programs. HotpotQA and HoVer are four-step programs; IFBench is two steps, a generator and a checker.
    • Feedback. Deliberately strong for the baseline: on training examples it names the correct answer (HotpotQA), the missing gold documents and the hop that lost them (HoVer), and each constraint's pass or fail (IFBench).
    • Metrics. HotpotQA is scored by mean F1, IFBench by prompt-level loose accuracy, HoVer by strict all-document retrieval.
    • Seeds. Three per arm, paired across arms.

    In HotpotQA seed 1, both runs selected the same parent candidate and chose to rewrite the same step, summarize1, still carrying its default instruction, "Given the fields question, passages, produce the fields summary." Both reflection calls saw the same inputs, outputs, and evaluator feedback.

    The judge run's call additionally saw two findings on the batch, Spurious_Fact_Introduced_At_Summarization and Dual_Summary_Aggregation_Failure, one of them quoting the evidence "Kapolei is colloquially known as being part of the leeward coast of Oahu."

    With feedback alone, the reflection model concluded that the summarizer was too cautious and rewrote toward confidence:

    Directly answer the question... Do not hedge or say the answer cannot be
determined if you can reasonably infer it from context or general knowledge
combined with the passages.

    With the judge's findings, it concluded that the summarizer was inventing content and rewrote toward grounding:

    Ground every claim strictly in the provided passages: Do NOT introduce any
facts, inferences, or details that are not explicitly stated in the passages.
...
Do not add colloquial nicknames, historical context, or additional facts
unless they are directly quoted or stated in one of the provided passages.

    The second rewrite even tracks the evidence: the judge had quoted a colloquial nickname, and the new instruction forbids exactly that. That failure mode, unsupported facts introduced at summarization, was also the judge's most frequent finding on HotpotQA, so the rewrite went after the program's most-reported problem.

    Integration point¶

    The judge plugs into GEPA through one optional argument on gepa.optimize, reflective_dataset_enricher. GEPA calls the enricher inside its reflective proposer, after the adapter has built the reflection records for the current batch and before the reflection model is asked for a revision. At that point the enricher reads the captured trajectories, runs the judge, and adds a failure_modes field to each diagnosed record. The adapter is never touched: DSPy, LangChain, and any custom adapter that follows GEPA's contract (reflection records aligned one-to-one with trajectories) keep their code, their evaluation, and their dataset construction. The reflection prompt gains one field, and leaving the argument out gives ordinary GEPA.

    Two things depend on what the adapter records. Step attribution needs the trajectories to expose per-step calls, as the adapters in the companion repository do; without them, findings are still produced but go to every step under revision. And what the judge sees depends on the adapter: with per-step calls it is shown only the task and each step's input, prompt, and output; without them it is shown the whole trajectory, so keeping evaluator output out of it is then the adapter's job.

    Using the enricher¶

    The enricher receives the candidate, the captured evaluation batch, the components under revision, and the adapter's reflection records, and returns those records with findings added; everything else in them is exactly what the adapter produced:

    import gepa

from failure_taxonomy import LLMFailureJudge, TaxonomyFeedbackEnricher, load_taxonomy

taxonomy = load_taxonomy("taxonomy.json")  # produced by the preparation pipeline, or your own
enricher = TaxonomyFeedbackEnricher(judge=LLMFailureJudge(taxonomy=taxonomy, lm=reflection_lm))

result = gepa.optimize(
    seed_candidate=seed,
    adapter=my_adapter,  # DSPy, LangChain, or custom, unchanged
    trainset=trainset,
    valset=valset,
    reflection_lm=reflection_lm,
    max_metric_calls=2000,  # or any other GEPA stop condition
    reflective_dataset_enricher=enricher,
)

    Availability¶

    The argument is available in gepa: pass it as shown above and nothing else about an adapter changes. The judge, the enricher, and the preparation pipeline are open source in the companion repository, together with a runnable IFBench demo of the whole pipeline.

    Next steps¶

    The evaluator kept its role in this study, and two studies follow from that division. The first is an ablation of the external call: a separate judge, compared with handing the full taxonomy to the reflection model and letting it apply the failure modes itself. The second is whether the judge can take the evaluator's role as well, serving both as the sole source of reflective feedback and as the rubric for ranking and accepting candidates, without gold labels or hand-written correctness feedback anywhere in the loop. That is the open question we are working on.
